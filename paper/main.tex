\begin{abstract}
Language-model approaches to single-cell data write a cell as a list of gene names ordered by expression and generate it token by token; this offers no likelihood on counts and, as we show, discards most of what such a likelihood measures. We introduce \cad, which treats a cell on a fixed gene panel as what its counts define, a probability distribution over genes, and fine-tunes a language model with a softmax decision head that returns this distribution from a text state in one forward pass. Cells are sampled from the decoded distribution, and every method is scored by the same exact multinomial likelihood of held-out counts. Rank sentences reconstruct log expression from rank with $R^2$ of $0.68$ to $0.74$, yet rewriting real cells as rank sentences keeps only $38\%$ of the achievable likelihood gain on held-out CRISPRa perturbations in K562, against $64$ to $73\%$ for mass-preserving formats; an autoregressive Cell2Sentence model fine-tuned on the same backbone and budget adds no likelihood over the perturbed mean, whereas \cad{} is $0.10$ nats per UMI better and decodes $90$ to $540$ times faster. On held-out perturbations \cad{} ties the strongest simple baseline in likelihood and on the top differentially expressed genes, and generates cells closer to real ones than that baseline; its per-cell gain comes from using the control cell as a residual prior on the output rather than as text in the prompt. On unseen PBMC donors it comes within $0.02$ nats per UMI of the cell-type mean, the optimum for a categorical state, and on unseen cell types it generates from type names alone, beating ontology-based baselines and matching a name-matching baseline in likelihood while recovering the requested type far more often. All comparisons use leakage-controlled splits and matched control cells, new configurations were promoted by rules fixed before the test runs, and we report ties and losses with paired intervals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Predicting how a cell will behave under a new condition, such as a different cell type or a genetic perturbation, is the central task of the ``virtual cell'' program~\citep{bunne2024virtualcell}.
Experiments cover only a tiny part of the space of conditions, so a model that can generate realistic cells for unmeasured conditions would make in silico screening and hypothesis testing possible.
Such a model must do more than predict an average profile: single cells from the same condition differ widely, and a faithful simulator has to reproduce this variation, which is why a generator should be judged by the likelihood it assigns to held-out cells.

Foundation models trained on millions of cells learn representations from expression tokens~\citep{cui2024scgpt,theodoris2023geneformer,rosen2023uce}, but they are trained to embed cells or predict masked genes and give no likelihood for a new cell.
Count models such as scVI~\citep{lopez2018scvi} do give one, but they condition on categorical labels rather than text.
Cell2Sentence (C2S) took a different route: it writes each cell as a sentence of gene names ordered by decreasing expression, so that a pretrained language model can be fine-tuned on cells and queried in natural language~\citep{levine2024cell2sentence,rizvi2025c2sscale}.
Its case for this representation is that expression can be recovered from rank by a log-linear fit with a high $R^2$.

We show that this criterion is misleading for generation.
A rank sentence assigns the same text to every cell with the same ordering, whatever its counts, and a language model trained on it assigns a probability to tokens rather than to a cell.
On our held-out cells the C2S rank-to-expression fit indeed reaches $R^2=0.68$ to $0.74$, yet for held-out perturbed cells the reconstructions keep only $38\%$ of the likelihood gain that the cells' own frequency vectors achieve (Section~\ref{sec:repr}).
High $R^2$ on log expression and a large loss of likelihood are compatible, because the rank of a gene is easy to recover while its share of the cell's counts is not.

We therefore represent a cell by what its counts define.
Fix a panel of $G$ genes: the counts divided by their sum form a probability vector on the gene simplex, and together with the library size this vector reproduces the counts exactly (Observation~\ref{prop:repr}).
A decoder that outputs a point on the simplex is a generative model with an exact likelihood, and a language model becomes such a decoder when its output layer is a softmax over the $G$ genes, a \emph{decision head} that answers a text prompt with one probability per gene in a single forward pass~\citep{unsloth_decision}.
We call the framework \cad{} (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).
We start from \backbone{}~\citep{c2sgemma_hf}, a Gemma-2 model~\citep{gemma2team2024} already trained on cell sentences, fine-tuned with low-rank adapters~\citep{hu2022lora}.
The idea of comparing cells as distributions over genes is not new~\citep{gmd2021}, and scVI also decodes gene frequencies; what is new is a text-conditioned decoder of this distribution whose likelihood is directly comparable with that of every other method.

We evaluate \cad{} where simple baselines are known to be hard to beat~\citep{ahlmann2025deep,wei2026benchmark}: generating cells of a given type for unseen donors (human PBMC) or of an unseen type, and generating the response to held-out CRISPRa perturbations in K562, under a gene-level split whose leakage audit found and removed a leak in an earlier version.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A likelihood interface for language-model cell generators.} A softmax decision head over a fixed gene panel turns a fine-tuned language model into a generator with an exact, normalized count likelihood in one forward pass, scored on the same scale as scVI and simple baselines (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A measured verdict on rank sentences.} Rank sentences look reversible by $R^2$ but keep $38\%$ of the likelihood gain against $64$ to $73\%$ for mass-preserving formats, and on the same backbone and budget autoregressive Cell2Sentence adds no likelihood over the perturbed mean while \cad{} gains $0.10$ nats per UMI at $90$ to $540$ times lower decoding cost (Sections~\ref{sec:repr} and~\ref{sec:backbone}).
\item \textbf{Where a text decoder helps, and where it does not.} \cad{} ties the strongest simple baseline on held-out perturbations; its per-cell gain comes from a control-cell residual on the output, not from reading the control cell; it generates unseen cell types from their names; and it cannot exceed the type mean for a categorical state, nor transfer knowledge to unseen genes (Sections~\ref{sec:pert}--\ref{sec:heads}).
\item \textbf{A protocol that exposes these differences.} Results by stratum of the split, perturbation-specific and differential-expression metrics next to likelihood, matched control cells across methods, and promotion rules written down before the test runs (Section~\ref{sec:tasks}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:background}
\paragraph{Language models and decision language models.}
Autoregressive language models generate text one token at a time; a \emph{decision language model} instead answers a typed question about a state by returning a probability over a bounded set of options in one pass~\citep{unsloth_decision}.
TypeSafe AI calls such models \emph{System One} models, which return typed outputs with probabilities from a single query instead of generating text~\citep{typesafe2026jev}; a third-party benchmark of its model Jev found its choice probabilities well calibrated and argued that answering a decision by generating and parsing text is indirect and leaves the answer's probability unavailable or unreliable~\citep{deusser2026jev}.
The open implementation we build on~\citep{unsloth_decision} attaches a classification-style head to the final hidden state of a pretrained model and trains it on soft targets with the cross-entropy, a proper scoring rule, so that the output is a probability vector to be scored rather than a sampled string to be parsed, which is exactly what a decoder of a gene distribution needs.

\paragraph{Cells as text.}
Cell2Sentence fine-tunes language models on rank-ordered gene sentences and has been scaled to billions of parameters and tens of millions of cells~\citep{levine2024cell2sentence,rizvi2025c2sscale}.
Other foundation models pretrain transformers on expression tokens (scGPT, Geneformer)~\citep{cui2024scgpt,theodoris2023geneformer} or learn zero-shot cell embeddings (UCE)~\citep{rosen2023uce}.
BMFM-RNA reconstructs the whole expression profile from a single bottleneck token and shows that whole-cell decoding improves representations~\citep{danziger2025bmfm}.
That objective is closest to ours in spirit, but it is self-supervised and its output is a continuous profile used to learn embeddings, whereas we decode a normalized simplex target from a text condition and evaluate it as a generative model.

\paragraph{Distributional generators of single cells.}
scVI models counts with a negative-binomial likelihood~\citep{lopez2018scvi}, scANVI adds cell-type labels~\citep{xu2021scanvi}, and scDiffusion samples cells with a conditional diffusion model~\citep{luo2024scdiffusion}.
They are the natural references for conditional generation, with likelihoods that account for overdispersion; the Gene Mover's Distance compares cells as distributions over genes~\citep{gmd2021}.

\paragraph{Perturbation response prediction.}
Neural predictors such as scGen, CPA and GEARS map a perturbation to an expression change~\citep{lotfollahi2019scgen,lotfollahi2023cpa,roohani2024gears}, and State predicts responses across cellular contexts~\citep{adduri2025state}.
A newer line models the whole perturbed population rather than a single profile: PopPert fits a low-rank Gaussian copula~\citep{wang2026poppert}, scDFM uses conditional flow matching with an MMD objective~\citep{yu2026scdfm}, and SCALE, TempoBridge, Departures and OCOO-T learn transport or flows between control and perturbed cells~\citep{chen2026scale,han2026tempobridge,chi2025departures,jiang2026ocoot}.
These methods operate on continuous expression or latent vectors and are evaluated mainly with errors on mean expression, differential-expression recovery and population distances.
Independent benchmarks report that mean, additive and linear baselines are hard to beat~\citep{ahlmann2025deep,wei2026benchmark} and that control-referenced metrics can be inflated by systematic variation~\citep{vinas2025systema}.
We therefore use a likelihood and the perturbed mean as references, and we compare with representative methods in Section~\ref{sec:pert}.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Problem setting and notation}
Let $G$ be the size of a fixed gene panel and let $x\in\mathbb{N}^G$ be the raw counts of one cell over the panel, with library size $N=\sum_gx_g$.
Each cell comes with a text \emph{state} $c$: a cell type for conditional generation, or a perturbation together with a control cell for perturbation response.
The goal is to learn a conditional model of cells given states that can be sampled and scored.
We use a panel of $G{=}2048$ genes fit on training cells only, so that no information from held-out donors or perturbations enters the representation: the genes with the largest dispersion of log-normalized expression among genes detected in more than $1\%$ of training cells (for Norman, the training perturbation targets are always included). The selected panels contain no ribosomal-protein (RPL/RPS) or mitochondrial genes.

\subsection{Why a distribution on a fixed gene panel}\label{sec:why}
The panel matters for the argument that follows: once the genes are fixed, the options of the decoder are fixed too, and one can ask which representation of $x$ keeps what a generative model needs.

\begin{observation}\label{prop:repr}
Let $x\in\mathbb{N}^G$ with $N=\sum_gx_g>0$, and let $p=x/N$.
(i) The map $x\mapsto(N,p)$ is injective, and a bijection onto $\{(N,p): N\in\mathbb{N}_{>0},\ p\in\Delta^{G-1},\ Np\in\mathbb{N}^G\}$, so the frequency vector together with the library size is lossless. Moreover, if $x\sim\mathrm{Mult}(N,\pi)$, then $p$ is the maximum-likelihood estimate of $\pi$ and the multinomial log-likelihood of $x$ under a model $q$ is $N\sum_gp_g\log q_g+\text{const}$, so $p$ is all that a scoring rule needs.
(ii) A rank sentence, the ordering of genes by $x$ with a fixed tie-breaking rule, is constant on every set of count vectors that share an ordering, so it is not injective and discards all expression mass.
(iii) Binarized vectors are not injective. Log-normalized or square-root vectors, once renormalized onto the simplex, define a distribution $p'$ that differs from $p$ unless all nonzero counts are equal, so a model trained to match $p'$ is not fit to the multinomial likelihood of $x$.
\end{observation}

Observation~\ref{prop:repr} (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}) states the choice made in this paper: on a fixed panel, the frequency vector is the representation under which a count-valued cell is both fully described and directly scorable.
The rank sentence of C2S is the representation that loses the most, because it keeps only the order of the genes and cannot be scored on counts without an additional reconstruction map.
Log-normalized targets are used in practice to stabilize training, but after renormalization they are a different distribution from $p$, so training on them no longer maximizes the likelihood of the counts; Section~\ref{sec:transform} and Appendix~\ref{app:transform} measure this trade-off.
Section~\ref{sec:results} measures the practical cost of the rank representation by rewriting real held-out cells as rank sentences, reconstructing them, and asking how much of the achievable likelihood gain survives.
The same section compares two input formats for the control cell on the same backbone.

\subsection{Transformations of the target}\label{sec:transform}
Only the training target changes with the transformation; evaluation always scores raw counts.
We consider four transformations $T$ that all land on the simplex $\Delta^{G-1}$: the \emph{frequency} $x/\sum x$; the \emph{smoothed} frequency $(x+0.1)/(\sum x+0.1G)$; \emph{log1p}, $\log(1{+}x)/\sum\log(1{+}x)$; and \emph{square root}, $\sqrt{x}/\sum\sqrt{x}$.
Frequency is the default because it is the maximum-likelihood point for the multinomial.
Log1p and square root flatten the distribution, which moves mass towards rarely expressed genes, and the smoothed target adds a uniform floor.
In our experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:transform}) the flattened targets cost $0.25$ to $0.29$ nats per UMI on Norman and worsen every sample-quality metric there, while on PBMC they trade $0.12$ to $0.14$ nats for a lower MMD; frequency is therefore the default throughout.
An scVI-normalized target, which would reuse a latent-space decoder, was not run.

\subsection{Decision language model decoder}\label{sec:decoder}
A text state $c$ is read by \backbone{} with rank-16 LoRA adapters on all linear layers~\citep{hu2022lora}.
The hidden state of the last token feeds a linear \emph{decision head} with one logit per panel gene, and a softmax gives $q_\theta(\cdot\mid c)\in\Delta^{G-1}$, exactly normalized.
Training minimizes the soft cross-entropy $-\sum_gp_g\log q_{\theta,g}$ between the transformed distribution $p=T(x)$ of a real cell and the decoded $q_\theta$.
For conditional generation, the state is the cell type.
For perturbation response, the state is the perturbation name together with a \emph{control cell} serialized as its 48 most abundant genes with a quantized log-mass level, a text that keeps mass information instead of only rank; each training perturbed cell is paired with a random control cell as in the C2S tutorial.
The head can carry a fixed log-prior added to the logits: none, the training mean, or the smoothed distribution of the control cell itself, which makes the model a residual on the control cell.
Because the head is initialized at zero, the model starts as exactly this prior and learns only a correction.
The per-cell model we recommend, called \emph{control residual} below, uses the control cell only through this prior and not in the prompt (Section~\ref{sec:residual}); its language-model output is then the same for every control cell of a perturbation, and all per-cell variation enters through $\log b$.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the pipeline.

\emph{Remark (what a categorical state can learn).} When the state is a cell type $c$, the training objective averaged over the training cells $\mathcal C_c$ of that type is $-\sum_g\bar p_{c,g}\log q_g$ with $\bar p_c=|\mathcal C_c|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal C_c}p_i$, so by Gibbs' inequality its unique minimizer on the simplex is the equal-weight mean $\bar p_c$. The pooled evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:likelihood} weights cells by library size and is minimized by the UMI-weighted mean $\sum_ix_i/\sum_iN_i$. A conditional decoder can therefore at best reproduce a type mean, and its two weightings differ whenever library size covaries with composition; a loss weighted by $N_i$ did not help on validation ($-0.0013$ nats per UMI, CI $-0.0047$ to $+0.0023$; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\subsection{Generation and likelihood}\label{sec:likelihood}
A cell is generated by drawing a library size $s$ from training cells and counts from $\mathrm{Mult}(s,q)$.
As a second shared sampler, a Dirichlet-multinomial with concentration $\kappa$ fit on training cells by the method of moments represents overdispersion, and all methods use the same sampler so that differences reflect the decoded distribution rather than the sampling step.
For a perturbation, every method receives the same $M=50$ held-out control cells, drawn with a seed derived from the perturbation's name.
Held-out cells are scored on their raw counts. For the held-out cells $x_1,\dots,x_n$ of a unit (library sizes $N_i$) and a model distribution $q$, we report the cross-entropy per UMI, $\ell(q)=-\sum_i\sum_gx_{ig}\log q_g/\sum_iN_i$. It equals the multinomial negative log-likelihood (NLL) per UMI up to the model-independent term $\sum_i[\log N_i!-\sum_g\log x_{ig}!]/\sum_iN_i$, so differences between models are exact differences in multinomial log-likelihood; we call it NLL for brevity.
A model that conditions on a control cell gives one distribution $q_m$ for each of $M$ control cells. The pooled NLL then uses $\bar q=\frac1M\sum_mq_m$, and the per-cell NLL is the mixture $-\sum_i\log\big(\frac1M\sum_m\exp\sum_gx_{ig}\log q_{mg}\big)/\sum_iN_i$, up to the same constant. For each cell this lies between the score of its best-matching control and that score plus $\log M$, that is, at most $\log M/N_i$ nats per UMI above it (about $0.02$ for $M{=}50$ at the median of about $216$ panel UMIs per K562 cell); a model without a control cell has one $q$, and its two scores coincide, except for the low-rank head of Section~\ref{sec:heads}, whose $q$ varies from cell to cell.
Because the log score is proper (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, P2), differences in NLL equal differences in KL divergence to the true expected distribution.
The multinomial likelihood ignores gene-specific overdispersion, so we also report a negative-binomial log-likelihood with gene-wise dispersions fit on training cells, using the mean $s\,q$ (Appendix~\ref{app:nb}); it leaves the ordering of the methods unchanged.

\section{Tasks, data and protocol}\label{sec:tasks}
\paragraph{Conditional generation.}
We use human PBMC from Hao et al.~\citep{hao2021seurat4} (CELLxGENE Census, 2025-11-08; after removing cells labeled \emph{unknown}, $161{,}159$ cells of 8 donors and $29$ cell types).
Donors P1--P5 are used for training, P6 for validation and P7--P8 for testing, so no cell of a held-out donor is in training.
The gene panel is fit on training donors only, and the units for statistics are cell types with at least 30 held-out cells.

\paragraph{Perturbation response.}
We use the CRISPRa screen of Norman et al.\ in K562~\citep{norman2019,scperturb2024} ($111{,}445$ cells; $105$ single and $131$ paired perturbations; $11{,}855$ controls).
In the \emph{gene-level split}, held-out single genes occur in no training perturbation.
An earlier split leaked ($7/10$ validation and $14/21$ test single genes occurred inside training pairs); an audit found the leak and all earlier results were discarded.
The $76$ test perturbations comprise three strata: $21$ unseen single genes, $29$ pairs of one unseen and one seen gene, and $26$ held-out pairs of two genes that were seen as single perturbations (validation: $10$, $25$ and $13$); pairs of two unseen genes are excluded by construction. Results are reported overall and per stratum.
We report a population-level analysis, where cells are pooled and the unit is the perturbation (with a gene-level 5-fold over $84$ single genes for power, Appendix~\ref{app:kfold}), and a \emph{per-cell} analysis, where a control cell goes in and a perturbed cell comes out.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
For conditional generation we use the global mean and the cell-type mean.
For perturbation response we use the control mean, the perturbed mean, a partial-additive baseline in which an unseen gene contributes nothing, ridge regression on gene embeddings, a copy of the control cell mixed with the perturbed mean (weight fit on training cells), and fine-tuned C2S following its tutorial (Section~\ref{sec:c2s}).
All baselines are in frequency space and share the sampler.
In addition, negative-binomial generators (scVI and scANVI, also sampled from their own negative binomial, and an scGen-style latent shift) and GEARS are compared in Sections~\ref{sec:pert} and~\ref{sec:condgen}, a non-language-model version of the control-cell residual isolates what the language model adds (Section~\ref{sec:residual}), and Cell2Sentence is also fine-tuned autoregressively on the same backbone and budget as \cad{} (Section~\ref{sec:c2s}); its generated sentences are mapped to frequencies by the C2S log-rank regression fit on training cells and mixed with the training perturbed mean, with the mixing weight chosen on validation from $\{0.01,0.03,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$.

\paragraph{Metrics and statistics.}
We report NLL per UMI; MMD with an RBF kernel and median heuristic, and real-versus-generated AUC from a logistic classifier with 5-fold cross-validation, both in a 50-dimensional PCA space fit on training cells; the Pearson correlation of gene means ($r_\mu$); and cell-type recovery by a classifier trained on real cells.
Likelihood weights genes by their mass, and a few genes carry much of it (TMSB4X alone accounts for about $16\%$ of panel UMIs in K562 control cells), so for perturbations we also measure the perturbation-specific change on pseudobulk profiles $\log(1+10^4\bar q)$: $r_\Delta$, the Pearson correlation between predicted and real change relative to the training perturbed centroid over all panel genes, which removes the effect shared by all perturbations~\citep{vinas2025systema}; and $r_{\mathrm{DE}}$, the same correlation relative to the held-out control mean on the unit's top-20 differentially expressed genes (largest $|t|$ of a Welch test between its real cells and held-out control cells).
The unit of analysis is the cell type or perturbation, and intervals are paired bootstraps over units.
Validation and folds guide all decisions, and the test split is evaluated once per configuration and seed; for the configurations beyond the main ones, the promotion rules were written down before they were run.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{A rank sentence looks reversible but discards most of the likelihood}\label{sec:repr}
\textbf{Objective.} Cell2Sentence motivates rank sentences by showing that expression can be reconstructed from rank with a high $R^2$~\citep{levine2024cell2sentence}. We ask whether the same holds for the quantity a generative model is judged by, the likelihood of held-out counts.

\textbf{Setup.} For each held-out unit we rewrote the \emph{real} test cells in each representation, reconstructed an expression profile from it (for rank sentences, the log-rank regression of C2S), and scored the pooled counts by multinomial NLL. The reference is the perturbed mean (Norman) or the cell-type mean (PBMC); the frequency vector of the same cells defines the achievable gain. To compare like with like, every representation, including the frequency vector, is estimated from the same subsample of cells with equal weight per cell and the same mixing weight fit on training cells; we also report the ratio against the pooled frequency oracle.

\textbf{Result.} By the C2S criterion (computed after the test runs) the rank sentence looks faithful: on our test cells the log-rank regression explains $R^2=0.68$ of log-normalized expression on Norman and $0.74$ on PBMC (Pearson $0.82$ and $0.87$). Yet on Norman it keeps only $38\%$ of the achievable likelihood gain ($36\%$ against the pooled oracle), whereas value bins keep $73\%$ ($69\%$) and our mass levels $64\%$ ($61\%$; Figure~\ref{fig:repr}b), and validation gives the same ordering. On PBMC the representations do not separate ($57$--$58\%$ of the matched gain each), because differences between cell types are carried mostly by which genes are expressed rather than by how much. A high $R^2$ on log expression and a large loss of likelihood are therefore compatible: the rank of a gene is reconstructed well, but its share of the cell's counts is not.

\textbf{The same holds for a trained model on the same backbone.}\label{sec:c2s} To separate the representation from the backbone, we fine-tuned C2S-Scale-Gemma-2-2B autoregressively on rank sentences with the same LoRA rank, cells, epochs and batch size as \cad{}, conditioned on the same control cells, and decoded its generated sentences with the C2S regression (mixing weight with the training mean chosen on validation; Section~\ref{sec:tasks}). The generated sentences are well formed ($115$ genes on average, $99.8\%$ valid gene names), but they add no likelihood beyond the perturbed mean ($-0.001$ nats per UMI, CI $-0.006$ to $+0.005$), and the selected mixing weight is the largest on the grid ($0.9$). \cad{} with the same backbone and budget is $0.103$ nats per UMI better (CI $0.071$ to $0.139$) and recovers far more of the perturbation-specific change ($r_\Delta=0.50$ against $0.11$). The setting of the C2S perturbation tutorial (C2S-Scale-Pythia-1B, full fine-tune, $500$ steps of $8$ pairs) is worse still, $0.224$ nats per UMI above the perturbed mean on validation (CI $+0.199$ to $+0.252$). In the reverse direction, the format of the \emph{input} matters little once the output is a distribution: giving the control cell as a 200-gene rank sentence instead of mass levels changes the test NLL by $-0.007$ (CI $-0.012$ to $+0.000$; earlier runs, with control cells not matched across methods) at $3.7$ to $4.3$ times the training time.

\subsection{Perturbation response: parity in likelihood, and where it comes from}\label{sec:pert}
\autoinput{auto_rev2/tab_norman_main.tex}
\textbf{Objective.} Simple baselines are hard to beat on held-out perturbations~\citep{ahlmann2025deep,wei2026benchmark}. We ask whether \cad{} matches the strongest of them in likelihood, in sample quality and in the perturbation-specific change, and in which regime of the split.

\textbf{Population level (Table~\ref{tab:norman_main}).} Without a control cell, \cad{} is $0.104$ nats per UMI better than the perturbed mean (CI $0.071$ to $0.139$), ties the additive baseline ($-0.009$, CI $-0.037$ to $+0.010$) and has a better pooled NLL than the scGen-style latent shift ($-0.016$, CI $-0.028$ to $-0.004$). Its generated cells are closer to real ones than those of the additive baseline (MMD $-0.013$, CI $-0.023$ to $-0.005$; AUC $-0.043$, CI $-0.057$ to $-0.029$) but not than those of scGen-style, which shifts every control cell individually. GEARS, trained with its default settings on the same split, is $0.466$ nats above the perturbed mean; its output is a regression of log-normalized expression that we map back to frequencies, and we did not tune it, so this row measures that conversion as much as the model.

\autoinput{auto_rev2/tab_strata.tex}
\textbf{By stratum (Table~\ref{tab:strata}).} The likelihood gain is concentrated where training contains the genes: on held-out pairs \cad{} is $0.108$ (one unseen gene) and $0.178$ (both genes seen) nats better than the perturbed mean, whereas on unseen single genes it ties it ($-0.006$, CI $-0.025$ to $+0.018$). Likelihood is dominated by high-mass genes, and the perturbation-specific metrics separate the methods differently: on pairs, \cad{}'s predicted change correlates slightly less with the real one than the additive baseline's over all panel genes ($r_\Delta$ lower by about $0.05$), while on each perturbation's top-20 DE genes it ties additive ($r_{\mathrm{DE}}$ $0.84$ and $0.90$) and exceeds scGen-style. The decoder thus reproduces the dominant transcriptional changes of known genes as well as additive composition does, but it does not yet capture the diffuse genome-wide part of the effect as well.

\textbf{Gene names, not gene knowledge.} Replacing gene symbols by opaque identifiers worsens the overall NLL ($+0.014$, CI $+0.002$ to $+0.027$) and lowers $r_\Delta$ in every stratum, as much on pairs of seen genes ($-0.043$, CI $-0.067$ to $-0.020$) as on unseen single genes ($-0.067$, CI $-0.126$ to $-0.007$), so names help the model organize what it learns from training perturbations; on unseen single genes the likelihood does not change ($+0.007$, CI $-0.022$ to $+0.030$). Adding NCBI gene summaries to the prompt did not help on validation. As in the population-level analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:kfold}), we find no evidence that the language model transfers knowledge to genes it has not seen perturbed. One visible reason is biological: CRISPRa raises the expression of its own target, which for the median unseen single gene, MAML2, is the most strongly increased of its top-20 DE genes (Figure~\ref{fig:demo}b), and a decoder that has never seen the gene perturbed has no way to infer this from the name alone.

\subsection{The per-cell gain comes from the control-cell residual}\label{sec:residual}
\textbf{Objective.} With the log-prior $b=\log\mathrm{smooth}(x_0)$, the zero-initialized head outputs exactly the control cell at the start of training and learns only a correction (Section~\ref{sec:decoder}). We ask whether the per-cell gain comes from this residual, from the language model reading the control cell as text, or from both.

\textbf{Result.} Removing the control-cell text while keeping the residual improves the per-cell NLL by $0.042$ (CI $0.035$ to $0.050$) and lowers MMD and AUC (Table~\ref{tab:norman_main}); keeping the text but removing the residual loses the per-cell gain ($+0.019$, CI $+0.012$ to $+0.027$). The residual model is $0.066$ nats per cell better than the additive baseline (CI $0.045$ to $0.095$) and matches scGen-style on MMD ($+0.001$, CI $-0.003$ to $+0.005$), although scGen-style keeps a lower per-cell NLL ($0.024$, CI $0.016$ to $0.032$). A version of the same residual without a language model (added after the test runs), in which the additive shift replaces the learned correction, reaches the same likelihood (pooled $-0.002$, CI $-0.028$ to $+0.016$; per cell $-0.005$, CI $-0.029$ to $+0.010$), but its cells are less realistic (MMD $+0.008$, CI $+0.001$ to $+0.018$; AUC $+0.030$, CI $+0.013$ to $+0.047$, both relative to \cad{}). The residual construction thus accounts for the per-cell likelihood gain, and what the language model adds on top is realism of the generated cells. The control cell is best used as a prior on the output rather than as text in the prompt: neither text form improves on the residual alone (per-cell NLL $5.171$ with mass-level text, $5.147$ with genes over-represented relative to the average control, $5.129$ without text).

\subsection{Conditional generation: a type mean for known types, composition of names for unseen ones}\label{sec:condgen}
\autoinput{auto_rev2/tab_pbmc.tex}
\textbf{Objective.} When the state is only a cell type, the Remark in Section~\ref{sec:decoder} says that the best a decoder can learn is the type mean. We test this on unseen donors, and then ask what the text interface buys when the type itself is unseen.

\textbf{Known types (Table~\ref{tab:pbmc_main}).} As predicted, no model improves on the cell-type mean in likelihood: \cad{} is slightly worse ($+0.017$ nats per UMI, CI $+0.001$ to $+0.036$), scVI and scANVI tie it, and on validation \cad{} gives the same NLL to four decimals for every LoRA rank and, at the same seed, for both backbones. On sample quality the decisive factor is the source of cell-to-cell variation, not the decoder: scVI and scANVI sampling from their own negative binomial from latent states of training cells reach the lowest MMD ($0.128$) and AUC ($0.924$) and a lower mixture NLL ($5.489$ against $5.606$), whereas every frequency decoder, ours included, relies on the shared sampler. A low-rank head that lets \cad{} sample its own variation lowers its mixture NLL to $5.552$ but does not reach scVI. No generator is close to indistinguishable from real cells of unseen donors (AUC $\ge0.92$; UMAP views in Appendix~\ref{app:umap}).

\textbf{Unseen types (Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot}).}
\autoinput{auto_rev2/tab_zeroshot.tex}
A model indexed by cell type cannot generate a type it has never seen; a decoder that reads the type's name can. In five leave-types-out folds over the $19$ PBMC types with enough cells, \cad{} trained on the remaining types generates each held-out type from its name and formal Cell Ontology definition (the pre-registered configuration). It is $0.707$ nats per UMI better than the global mean (CI $0.545$ to $0.862$), $0.498$ better than the nearest training type in the Cell Ontology (CI $0.146$ to $0.984$), and $0.192$ better than the pooled training types under the held-out type's ontology parents (CI $0.045$ to $0.352$; this and the next baseline were added after the test runs). It ties a string-matching baseline that copies the training type whose name shares the most words ($-0.058$, CI $-0.219$ to $+0.101$), but it recovers the requested type far more often ($0.138$ against $0.022$). The name alone gives the same likelihood as name plus definition (difference below $0.001$ nats), and replacing names by opaque identifiers removes the gain entirely. The decoder therefore generalizes by composing the words of type names; we find no evidence that it uses knowledge beyond the names.

\subsection{Distributional heads: each buys one property at the cost of another}\label{sec:heads}
\textbf{Objective.} A flat softmax gives one distribution per state, so cell-to-cell variation and gene-specific dispersion come only from the shared sampler (Section~\ref{sec:likelihood}). We tested two heads that keep the exact normalization of $q$ and the single language-model pass, each promoted from validation by the pre-written rules.

\textbf{Negative-binomial head.} The head adds a per-gene inverse dispersion $\theta_g(c)$ and is trained on the negative-binomial likelihood of the counts with mean $N_iq_g$, so genes with high mass no longer dominate the gradient. On the Norman test split (no control cell, against the softmax head) it raises the perturbation-specific correlation $r_\Delta$ by $0.020$ (CI $0.012$ to $0.029$), and by $0.049$ on unseen single genes (CI $0.034$ to $0.067$), with a slightly lower MMD and AUC, at a small cost in pooled multinomial NLL ($+0.015$, CI $+0.006$ to $+0.028$). On PBMC, where the state is categorical, it did not help on validation and was not promoted.

\textbf{Low-rank logistic-normal head.} The logits become $z=Wh+\log b+U\,\mathrm{diag}(a(h))\,\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I_{16})$, so $q$ itself varies from cell to cell along $16$ learned gene programs; training uses a variational bound with an encoder of the target cell, and generation draws $\epsilon$ from the prior. Without seeing any control cell, it lowers the per-cell NLL by $0.089$ (CI $0.080$ to $0.098$) on Norman, and on PBMC from $5.623$ to $5.552$ (Table~\ref{tab:pbmc_main}), reaching the per-cell NLL of the scGen-style model, which encodes every control cell ($-0.009$, CI $-0.019$ to $+0.002$). The price is a worse pooled NLL of the mean over draws ($+0.062$, CI $+0.040$ to $+0.087$) and a lower $r_\Delta$ ($-0.067$, CI $-0.092$ to $-0.042$). The per-cell NLL is estimated from $50$ (Norman) or $300$ (PBMC) prior draws per state; this log-mean-exp estimator is biased upwards, so the gain is if anything understated.

\subsection{Cost and robustness}\label{sec:backbone}
\textbf{One pass instead of hundreds of tokens.} Because the head returns the whole distribution at once, decoding costs one forward pass of the prompt. Measured after the test runs, alone on one A100 with the same backbone and batch size ($25$), C2S-Gemma needs $1.99$ seconds to generate one cell sentence ($530$ new tokens on average), whereas \cad{} needs $0.004$ seconds per cell with the control cell as a residual and $0.022$ seconds when the control cell is also written as $342$ tokens of text, that is, $90$ to $540$ times less; without a control cell, one pass of $0.004$ seconds gives the distribution for every cell of that state. Training on the same cells is also cheaper: $13$ minutes for \cad{} against $128$ minutes for C2S-Gemma, although these wall-clock times were measured with other jobs on the GPU.

\textbf{Backbone, seeds and adapter rank.} We compare the general-purpose Qwen3.5-0.8B with the Cell2Sentence-initialized C2S-Scale-Gemma-2-2B~\citep{c2sgemma_hf,gemma2team2024} under the same head, LoRA rank and budget, with three seeds each on test. Gemma is consistently but modestly better: the pooled NLL is $5.6272$ against $5.6394$ for conditional generation and $5.1848$ against $5.1958$ for perturbation response without a control cell, a gap of $0.011$ to $0.012$ nats that is present for every seed and exceeds the seed standard deviation ($0.001$ to $0.003$), yet does not change any ranking against the baselines. On validation the backbone made no measurable difference, and LoRA ranks $8$, $16$ and $64$ give the same NLL to within $0.002$ nats. Across seeds the control-residual model varies by at most $0.004$ in per-cell NLL ($5.127$ to $5.130$).

\section{Conclusion and discussion}
Writing a cell as what its counts define, a distribution over a fixed panel, turns a language model into a cell generator with an exact likelihood: a softmax decision head returns the whole distribution in one pass, and the same multinomial score applies to every method.
This interface makes the representation a measurable choice, much as decision models for text are evaluated not only on the chosen option but on the calibration of its probabilities~\citep{deusser2026jev}; for a cell, the held-out count likelihood plays that role.
Rank sentences look reversible by the criterion used to motivate them, yet they lose most of the likelihood that a distribution keeps, and on the same backbone and budget an autoregressive rank-sentence model adds no likelihood over the perturbed mean while costing two orders of magnitude more to decode.
As a model, \cad{} is as good as the strongest simple baseline on held-out perturbations, generates more realistic cells than that baseline when the control cell enters as a residual prior, and generates unseen cell types from their names.
Its limits are equally clear and, we argue, informative: a categorical state can only reach its training mean, the language model's gene knowledge does not transfer to genes it has not seen perturbed, and the diffuse genome-wide part of a perturbation is still captured better by additive composition.
These limits point to what the next text-conditioned cell model needs: richer states than a label (donor, cell-level and pathway context), output heads that model cell-to-cell covariance and dispersion together, as our negative-binomial and low-rank heads begin to do separately, and evaluation by stratum and by perturbation-specific change rather than by a single pooled number.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
There is one dataset per task, a $2048$-gene panel, and CRISPRa genetic perturbations only; drug and dose responses were not studied.
Held-out control cells are not paired with the perturbed cells, so per-cell scores reward covering the distribution of cell states rather than predicting a counterfactual.
The multinomial likelihood ignores gene-specific overdispersion (a negative-binomial evaluation leaves the rankings unchanged, Appendix~\ref{app:nb}), and the flat head models neither dispersion nor gene--gene covariance; the two new heads each address one of these at a cost.
GEARS was run with default settings and its regression output converted to frequencies, and CPA and the transport and flow models of Section~\ref{sec:background} were not compared, because they do not output a distribution over a fixed gene list.
Zero-shot cell types were evaluated with the Qwen backbone and five folds over $19$ types.
Each test configuration was evaluated once per seed: Qwen seed~0 was first evaluated before the Gemma runs, Gemma was chosen as the main backbone before its test results were known, the configurations beyond the main ones were promoted by rules written down before their test runs (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}), and three zero-shot validation runs that failed for lack of memory were re-run after the zero-shot gate had been applied to the remaining two folds; on all five folds the gate gives the same decision.

\paragraph{Code availability.} Code, held-out unit lists, run scripts and the table-generation scripts are available, with a README, at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/CellAsDistribution-R27}.
\paragraph{AI use statement.} Code, experiments, tables, figures and a first draft of this text were produced with assistance from Claude (Anthropic); the authors reviewed all content and are responsible for it.

\paragraph{scVI and scANVI.}
Both are trained on training cells of the panel with a negative-binomial likelihood, $30$ latent dimensions and $60$ epochs (scANVI: $15$ further epochs with cell-type labels).
The frequency vector of a cell type is the mean decoder output over up to $3000$ training cells of that type.

\paragraph{scGen-style baseline.}
An scVI model is trained on all training cells.
The latent shift of a single gene is the mean latent state of its perturbed cells minus that of the control cells, the shift of a pair is the sum of the shifts of its known genes, and a perturbation with no known gene uses the mean shift of all training perturbations.
Each held-out control cell is encoded, shifted and decoded with library size one.

\section{Negative-binomial evaluation}\label{app:nb}
For a held-out cell $i$ of a unit with library size $s_i$ and model frequency $q$, the count of gene $g$ is scored as $\mathrm{NB}(x_{ig}\mid\mu=s_iq_g,\phi_g)$ with variance $\mu+\phi_g\mu^2$.
The dispersions $\phi_g$ come from a method-of-moments fit on library-normalized training counts with the within-unit variance pooled over units (median $\phi=26.5$ for PBMC and $9.0$ for Norman, clipped to $[10^{-3},10^2]$).
The score is the negative log-likelihood per UMI, which is on a different scale from the multinomial score because it includes the zeros, so only differences are compared (Table~\ref{tab:nb}).
It uses the pooled frequency of a unit, so it does not reward conditioning on a control cell.

\section{Target transformations}\label{app:transform}
\autoinput{auto/pbmc_val_transform_table.tex}
Table~\ref{tab:pbmc_val_transform} (validation, $19$ cell types, Qwen3.5-0.8B backbone) shows that the transformation trades likelihood for sample quality on PBMC.
Frequency gives the best NLL, and smoothing is worse on every metric ($+0.172$ nats, 95\% CI $+0.144$ to $+0.196$; MMD $+0.074$).
Log1p and square-root targets are $+0.144$ and $+0.118$ nats worse in NLL, yet their MMD is lower ($-0.057$, CI $-0.107$ to $-0.016$, and $-0.057$, CI $-0.109$ to $-0.014$) with unchanged real-versus-generated AUC.
A flatter target moves mass to rarely expressed genes, which lowers the likelihood of raw counts while placing generated cells closer to real ones in PCA space.

For perturbation response the trade-off does not appear (Gemma backbone, test, $76$ perturbations; Appendix~\ref{app:val}).
Relative to the frequency target, log1p is $0.288$ nats worse in NLL (CI $+0.249$ to $+0.333$) and square root $0.250$ worse (CI $+0.219$ to $+0.282$), and both also have a higher MMD ($0.094$ and $0.087$ against $0.039$) and a higher real-versus-generated AUC ($0.875$ and $0.871$ against $0.811$).
We therefore keep frequency as the default, and we report the sample-quality advantage of flattened targets as specific to the conditional PBMC task in our experiments.

\section{Decision rules and the test-split record}\label{app:protocol}
Beyond the main configurations, every variant was first run on validation (Qwen3.5-0.8B backbone, seed~0, matched control cells, comparators re-run with the same control cells) and promoted to the test split only if, on validation, its paired 95\% interval against its comparator excluded zero in its favor on at least one metric fixed in advance, and no such metric was worse with an interval excluding zero; at most three new configurations were promoted. \section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
\textbf{Observation~\ref{prop:repr}.} (i) $x=Np$ recovers the counts from $(N,p)$, and $(N,p)$ is a function of $x$. Under $\mathrm{Mult}(N,q)$, $\log P(x)=\log\frac{N!}{\prod_gx_g!}+\sum_gx_g\log q_g=\text{const}+N\sum_gp_g\log q_g$, which by Gibbs' inequality is maximized over $q\in\Delta$ at $q=p$. (ii) For any $x$ with at least two distinct nonzero counts, $x$ and $2x$ have the same ordering and hence the same sentence, and so do $x$ and any count vector obtained by a strictly increasing change of the counts. (iii) $x$ and $2x$ have the same binarization. For $f(t)=\log(1+t)$ or $f(t)=\sqrt t$, the renormalized vector is $p'_g=f(x_g)/\sum_hf(x_h)$, and $p'=p$ requires $f(x_g)/x_g$ to be equal for all genes with $x_g>0$; because $f(t)/t$ is strictly decreasing for $t>0$, this holds only when all nonzero counts are equal.

Throughout, $\Delta$ is the simplex over the $G$ panel genes and modules $S_1,\dots,S_M$ partition the genes; P1, P3 and P4 concern the hierarchical head of Appendix~\ref{app:tree}, and P2 concerns the NLL used throughout.
\begin{description}
\item[P1 (normalization and exact factorization).] For any $q^{\mathrm{root}}\in\Delta^{M}$ and $q^{m}\in\Delta^{|S_m|}$, $q(g)=q^{\mathrm{root}}(m(g))\,q^{m(g)}(g)$ lies in $\Delta$, and for counts $X$ with module totals $n_m$,
\[-\sum_gX_g\log q(g)=-\sum_mn_m\log q^{\mathrm{root}}(m)-\sum_m\sum_{g\in S_m}X_g\log q^m(g).\]
Every $q$ in the interior of $\Delta$ factors uniquely this way, so the hierarchical head is a \emph{re-parametrization} of the flat head: it changes what the network must output, not the family of distributions it can represent.
\item[P2 (the metric is proper).] If the counts $X$ of a perturbation's cells satisfy $\mathbb{E}[X_g/\sum_hX_h]=\pi_g$ given the total, the expected NLL per UMI of $q$ is $H(\pi)+\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,q)$ and is uniquely minimized at $q=\pi$. Differences in expected NLL between two decoders therefore equal differences in $\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|q)$.
\item[P3 (tilt identity and its population minimizer).] For $f,\tilde p$ in the interior of $\Delta$, $q_g\propto f_gt_g$ equals $\tilde p_g$ exactly. If a context is paired with bags $b$ having tilts $t_b$, the minimizer of the expected soft cross-entropy is $\mathbb{E}[t_b]$, so the implied decoder is $q_g\propto\mathbb{E}_b[\tilde p_{b,g}/Z_b]$: a $1/Z_b$-weighted average of bag frequencies, which equals the plain average whenever $Z_b$ is constant within a context. The target is the shrunk $\tilde p$, so $\lVert\tilde p-p\rVert_1\le\tfrac{\lambda}{n+\lambda}\lVert f-p\rVert_1\le2\tfrac{\lambda}{n+\lambda}$ ($\approx1\%$ for $n\approx2\!\times\!10^5$ UMIs per bag).
\item[P4 (sampled-leaf loss).] Let $T_m=\sum_{g\in S_m}t_g$. With modules $A=(m_1,\dots,m_R)$ drawn i.i.d.\ from $T$, $\mathrm{CE}(T,r^{\mathrm{root}})+\mathbb{E}\big[\tfrac1R\sum_{r}\mathrm{CE}(t|_{m_r},r^{m_r})\big]=\mathrm{CE}(t,r)$, the full tree cross-entropy. \emph{Our implementation samples without replacement and the trainer averages questions with equal weight, so its record loss is a weighted surrogate} ($1/(R{+}1)$ per question), not this unbiased estimator; the two share their minimizer only when every conditional is exactly representable (the well-specified regime).
\end{description}
\textbf{P1.} $\sum_gq(g)=\sum_mq^{\mathrm{root}}(m)\sum_{g\in S_m}q^m(g)=\sum_mq^{\mathrm{root}}(m)=1$ and every factor is nonnegative. The loss identity follows from $\log q(g)=\log q^{\mathrm{root}}(m(g))+\log q^{m(g)}(g)$ and $\sum_{g\in S_m}X_g=n_m$. Conversely, for $q$ in the interior set $q^{\mathrm{root}}(m)=\sum_{g\in S_m}q(g)$ and $q^m(g)=q(g)/q^{\mathrm{root}}(m)$; these are the only factors that reproduce $q$ because $q^{\mathrm{root}}(m)=\sum_{g\in S_m}q(g)$ is forced by summing.

\textbf{P2.} Conditional on the total $N$, $\mathbb{E}[-\sum_gX_g\log q_g/N]=-\sum_g\pi_g\log q_g=H(\pi)+\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|q)$, and $\mathrm{KL}\ge0$ with equality if and only if $q=\pi$ (Gibbs' inequality). Two decoders therefore differ in expected NLL by $\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|q_A)-\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|q_B)$. Per-UMI scoring weights each perturbation's cells by their UMIs; the paired bootstrap treats the perturbation as the unit.

\textbf{P3.} $f_gt_g=\tilde p_g/Z$, so $\sum_hf_ht_h=1/Z$ and $f_gt_g/\sum_hf_ht_h=\tilde p_g$. For targets $t_b$ drawn with context $c$, $\mathbb{E}_b[\mathrm{CE}(t_b,r)]=H(\mathbb{E}t_b)+\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{E}t_b\|r)+\text{const}$ is minimized by $r=\mathbb{E}[t_b]$, so $q_g\propto f_g\mathbb{E}[t_{b,g}]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde p_{b,g}/Z_b]$, a $1/Z_b$-weighted mean of $\tilde p_b$. With $\tilde p=(1-w)p+wf$ and $w=\lambda/(n+\lambda)$, $\lVert\tilde p-p\rVert_1=w\lVert f-p\rVert_1\le2w$.

\textbf{P4.} The chain rule gives $\mathrm{CE}(t,r)=\mathrm{CE}(T,r^{\mathrm{root}})+\sum_mT_m\,\mathrm{CE}(t|_m,r^m)$ with $t|_m=t_{S_m}/T_m$. For $m_1,\dots,m_R$ i.i.d.\ from $T$, $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{CE}(t|_{m_r},r^{m_r})]=\sum_mT_m\mathrm{CE}(t|_m,r^m)$ for each $r$, which gives the claim. Sampling without replacement changes the inclusion probabilities away from $T_m$, and averaging over the $R{+}1$ answered questions gives each question weight $1/(R{+}1)$ instead of $(1,\tfrac1R,\dots,\tfrac1R)$; if every conditional is representable, each term is minimized at the true conditional and the minimizer is unchanged.
\section{Hierarchical head ablation}\label{app:tree}
The hierarchical variant (\cadn{}/\cadr{}) answers one 64-option module question and four 32-option gene questions through the Unsloth decision head, trained on a \emph{tilt} target (the ratio of the perturbed to the control frequency, renormalized, with the control prior $f$ multiplied back at generation). On the corrected Norman split, trained on absolute frequencies it is $+0.161$ [$+0.150$, $+0.174$] nats worse than the perturbed mean (control-state NLL $5.490$ against $5.295$ for the control frequencies); the tilt repairs the fit (control $5.300$; tilt minus no-tilt $-0.138$ [$-0.157$, $-0.119$]) but the tilted head remains $+0.023$ [$+0.009$, $+0.038$] worse than the perturbed mean and $0.100$ nats worse than the flat head. The biological partition beats a random one by $0.019$ ($[-0.035,-0.002]$). We therefore use the flat head in the main text.
