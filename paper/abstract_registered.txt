TITLE: CellAsDistribution: Decision-Native Language Modeling of Single-Cell Gene Composition

ABSTRACT (plain text for EasyChair; must match paper/main.tex):
Language models of single cells write a cell as a sentence of gene names ordered by expression and generate it token by token, so their output is text that must be mapped back to counts before it can be scored. We propose instead to let a decision language model generate the cell directly: on a fixed gene panel, a cell's counts define a probability distribution over genes, and a softmax decision head on a fine-tuned language model returns this distribution from a text description of the cell's state in one forward pass. The output is the expected share of the cell's transcripts coming from each gene; it is normalized by construction and scored by the multinomial likelihood of held-out counts, on the same scale as every other method. On held-out CRISPRa perturbations in K562, the decoder is 0.10 nats per UMI better than an autoregressive Cell2Sentence model fine-tuned on the same backbone and budget, which adds no likelihood over the perturbed mean, and it decodes 90 to 540 times faster. Rewriting real cells as rank sentences shows that much of what a sentence loses lies in the map back to counts: the published reconstruction keeps 38% of the achievable likelihood gain, a map learned from training cells 88%; yet re-decoding the model's generated sentences with that learned map does not close the gap. In pre-registered controls, a decoder built from learned gene embeddings with the same softmax output matches the language model in likelihood, and a frozen language model is worse, so the gain comes from decoding the distribution directly; the fine-tuned language model contributes the text interface. Biologically, the decoder ties additive composition of known perturbation effects in likelihood and on the downstream differentially expressed genes with the targeted genes excluded, generates cells closer to real ones, and does not transfer to genes never perturbed in training. For cell types it reaches, but cannot exceed, the type mean on unseen donors, and it generates unseen types from their names, matching a name-matching baseline in likelihood while recovering the requested type far more often. All comparisons use leakage-controlled splits and matched control cells; new configurations were promoted by rules fixed before the test runs, and analyses added afterwards are labeled as such.
