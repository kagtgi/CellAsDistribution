TITLE: Why Generate a Sentence? A Decision Language Model for Modeling Single-Cell Composition

ABSTRACT (plain text for EasyChair; must match paper/main.tex):
Language models of single cells write a cell as a sentence of gene names ordered by expression and generate it token by token, so their output is text that must be mapped back to counts before it can be scored. We ask whether a sentence is needed at all. On a fixed gene panel a cell’s counts define a probability distribution over genes, and a decision head, a softmax over the panel on the final state of a condition encoder, returns this distribution from a text description of the cell’s state in one forward pass; every method is then scored by the multinomial likelihood of held-out counts, on one proper scale. We use this decision-native interface to take apart text-based cell generation on human PBMC and on held-out CRISPRa perturbations in K562. On the same backbone and budget, decoding the distribution is 0.10 nats per UMI better than autoregressive Cell2Sentence, which adds no likelihood over the perturbed mean, recovers downstream differential expression far better (r = 0.77 against 0.56) and decodes 90 to 540 times faster. Rewriting real cells shows that the published rank-to-count map keeps 38% of the achievable likelihood gain and a map learned from training cells 88%, yet generated sentences stay far behind under either map. Pre-registered controls locate the gain: a decoder built from learned gene embeddings matches the fine-tuned language model on perturbations, a frozen language model is worse, and neither text-derived (GenePT) nor co-expression gene embeddings transfer to genes never perturbed in training. Biologically, the decoder ties additive composition on likelihood and on downstream genes with the targeted genes excluded; it shrinks the induction of the targeted gene, which an explicit target prior removes; and no learned model predicts the self-induction of a gene it has never seen perturbed. The language model earns its place in the text interface: with a backbone pretrained on cell sentences, it generates unseen cell types from their names better than copying the training type whose name shares the most words. All comparisons use leakage-controlled splits and matched control cells; new configurations were promoted by rules fixed before the test runs, and analyses added afterwards are labeled as such.
